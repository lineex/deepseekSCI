# Feasibility and routing

## Purpose

The pilot determines whether a review question is novel, answerable, extractable and operationally feasible. It does not estimate the final effect and must not be used to select favorable outcomes.

## Pilot sequence

1. Write a provisional question and candidate estimand.
2. Search recent systematic reviews, protocols and guidelines.
3. Identify two to five known core studies.
4. Run a broad query and screen a deterministic sample, such as the first 50 results sorted by date plus all known studies.
5. Run a focused query to test precision.
6. Retrieve a small full-text set spanning likely designs and outcomes.
7. Test whether the planned extraction fields can be completed.
8. Build a provisional synthesis-set table without calculating a pooled effect.

## Feasibility domains

Score each domain `0`, `1` or `2` and justify it in prose. Scores support judgment; they do not replace it.

| Domain | 0 | 1 | 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Novelty | current high-quality review answers the same question | update or narrower gap plausible | clear unresolved question |
| Clinical/research value | no decision or knowledge consequence | mainly descriptive value | result would change interpretation, practice or trial design |
| Question clarity | unstable population/exposure/outcome | partly specifiable | one primary estimand is explicit |
| Evidence density | no eligible studies | sparse evidence | adequate studies for systematic synthesis |
| Meta comparability | no coherent pair | one fragile candidate set | at least one plausible coherent synthesis set |
| Data extractability | essential values unavailable | author contact/transformation likely | reproducible effect inputs available |
| Bias interpretability | designs cannot support intended claim | major limitations manageable | appropriate designs and appraisal available |
| Operational feasibility | access/team/time absent | one major dependency | full text, reviewers, methods and time available |

## Route rules

### Systematic review with meta-analysis

Choose when:

- the question and primary estimand are stable;
- exhaustive study identification is necessary;
- at least two studies may estimate the same clinically meaningful contrast;
- effect inputs can be reproduced;
- dependence and heterogeneity can be handled credibly.

The final review may still end without a pooled estimate if full-text evidence invalidates comparability.

### Systematic review without meta-analysis

Choose when exhaustive identification is important but populations, exposures, outcomes or estimands prevent defensible pooling. Follow SWiM or another appropriate synthesis framework.

### Scoping review

Choose when mapping definitions, methods, concepts, evidence breadth or research gaps is the primary objective. Do not use a scoping label to avoid critical appraisal when the question is actually about effectiveness.

### Narrow review

Choose when a focused empirical contradiction or translational problem remains important but the literature is too sparse, indirect or heterogeneous for exhaustive effect estimation. Preserve transparent search and study-selection methods, but avoid prevalence claims unless screening is complete.

### Retarget or stop

Choose when the topic is redundant, the question is not answerable, the required evidence is inaccessible, or the result would have little consequence. A failed feasibility pilot is useful output; it prevents a weak review.

## Feasibility lock

Record:

- date and search interfaces;
- exact pilot queries;
- sample selection rule;
- known studies recovered or missed;
- overlap with existing reviews and their search end dates;
- provisional eligible count and design mix;
- candidate synthesis sets;
- route decision and fallback route;
- conditions that would reverse the decision.
