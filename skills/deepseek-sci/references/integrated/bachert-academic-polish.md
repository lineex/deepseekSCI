# Bachert Academic Polish

Use this skill to revise biomedical or clinical-academic writing into a style inspired by high-level rhinology / mucosal immunology literature associated with authors such as Claus Bachert.

## Core boundary

Do **not** imitate, impersonate, or claim to reproduce the exact personal style of any living scholar. Instead, extract **high-level domain writing traits** common to strong papers in this field:
- precise terminology
- cautious causal language
- mechanism-to-clinic linkage
- restrained tone
- reduced AI-like generic phrasing

## Primary goal

Revise the user’s manuscript so it sounds like a mature, field-aware academic author wrote it, especially in topics such as:
- chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps (CRSwNP)
- allergic rhinitis (AR)
- nasal mucosal immunology
- type 2 inflammation
- epithelial barrier dysfunction
- eosinophilic inflammation
- biologics and targeted therapy

## Writing characteristics to preserve

1. **Problem-oriented framing**
   - Define disease burden, inflammatory pattern, or unmet clinical need early.
   - Prefer concrete framing over broad introductory filler.

2. **Mechanism-clinic connection**
   - Link immunopathology with phenotype, recurrence, severity, or treatment response.
   - Connect observations to endotypes when appropriate.

3. **Restrained scholarly tone**
   - Prefer: "is characterized by", "is associated with", "appears to contribute to", "current evidence suggests".
   - Avoid overstatement and promotional language.

4. **Evidence-aware wording**
   - Keep causal claims narrower than the data supports.
   - Use calibrated uncertainty when needed.

5. **Dense but readable sentences**
   - Favor clear, moderate-length sentences.
   - Remove repetitive transitions and formulaic AI phrasing.

## Remove AI-like signals

Actively reduce the following:
- empty opening phrases such as "with the continuous deepening of research"
- exaggerated significance claims such as "greatly important", "highly innovative", "fully proves"
- overly symmetrical list structures when they add no substance
- repetitive template transitions: "not only... but also... meanwhile... in addition..."
- vague buzzwords without mechanism or evidence
- conclusion-first paragraphs that do not build reasoning

## Revision workflow

When the user provides a paragraph or section:

1. Identify the text type:
   - introduction / background
   - mechanism discussion
   - results narration
   - discussion / interpretation
   - conclusion / abstract

2. Preserve the scientific meaning.

3. Revise for:
   - terminology accuracy
   - logical progression
   - less formulaic phrasing
   - stronger mechanism-clinic linkage
   - more publication-ready tone

4. If claims are too strong, soften them without making them vague.

5. If the paragraph is too generic, make it more field-specific using domain-appropriate concepts already supported by the text.

## Style rules for English output

- Use formal biomedical English.
- Avoid inflated novelty claims.
- Prefer precise verbs over decorative adjectives.
- Keep paragraph logic: background → mechanism → implication.
- Avoid sounding machine-generated by varying sentence openings naturally.

## Style rules for Chinese output

- Avoid policy-report tone and empty rhetoric.
- Prefer concise academic Chinese over translated-English stiffness.
- Replace broad statements with disease- or mechanism-specific expressions.
- Maintain moderation and evidentiary discipline.

## What not to do

- Do not fabricate references, data, or expert opinions.
- Do not insert claims not inferable from the provided text.
- Do not imitate a living author’s unique phrasing.
- Do not over-humanize by making the text casual.
- Do not flatten all sentences into the same rhythm.

## Example transformations

### Example 1
Original:
"近年来，越来越多研究表明该病机制复杂，并且与多种炎症因子密切相关，因此精准治疗非常重要。"

Better:
"越来越多的证据提示，该病具有明显的生物学异质性，并受到多条炎症通路的共同驱动。对相关炎症机制的进一步界定，可能有助于支持更具针对性的治疗策略。"

### Example 2
Original:
"CRSwNP usually causes serious clinical problems and has a very complex mechanism."

Better:
"CRSwNP is a heterogeneous inflammatory disorder associated with substantial disease burden, and its pathophysiology involves a complex interaction between epithelial dysfunction, local immune responses, and persistent inflammatory signaling."

## Blueprint v2 integrated execution protocol

This section operationalizes the skill using the agreed multi-agent framework.

### Layer A — Evidence layer (must run first for high-stakes edits)

Before rewriting high-impact sections (Abstract, Discussion, Conclusion), build or request an evidence table with at least:
- topic
- key claim
- evidence level (guideline/RCT/cohort/case/expert opinion)
- recommended wording strength
- forbidden wording
- citation anchor (PMID/DOI if available)

**Rule:** do not apply strong causal wording unless the evidence row supports it.

### Layer B — Clinical reasoning layer (5-dimension rubric)

Score each paragraph on a 1–5 scale across the following weighted dimensions:
- Terminology accuracy: **25%**
- Evidence–wording alignment: **30%**
- Mechanism-to-clinical linkage: **20%**
- Causal caution: **15%**
- Extrapolation boundary control: **10%**

Weighted score:
`0.25*T + 0.30*E + 0.20*M + 0.15*C + 0.10*X`

**Passing threshold:** weighted score >= 3.5.

If requested, report weak dimensions and fix them in revision pass 2.

### Layer C — Style policy layer (domain convention extraction only)

The style transfer must follow this declaration:
**Domain Writing Convention Extraction Only**

Enforce these five policy groups:
1. De-hype language (remove promotional adjectives and certainty inflation)
2. Hedging by evidence level (insert/remove uncertainty markers appropriately)
3. Mechanism bridge requirement in interpretation-heavy paragraphs
4. Template sentence suppression (avoid repetitive LLM scaffolds)
5. Terminology normalization (first full term, then consistent abbreviation)

### Layer D — Polish engine (two-pass rewriting)

Perform rewrites in two passes:
1. **Structural pass** — paragraph logic, argument flow, evidence ordering
2. **Sentence pass** — diction, hedging, concision, rhythm, cohesion

Provide one or more outputs as needed:
- clean version
- tracked-change style comparison
- rationale notes (why key lines changed)

### Layer E — Validation loop (quality gate)

After polishing, run a self-check against these targets:
- expert-style quality improvement target: +0.8 (Likert-like internal estimate)
- terminology error reduction target: >=40%
- overclaim reduction target: >=50%
- template sentence ratio target: <=8% (stretch goal <=2 per 1000 words)

If the text still contains overclaim triggers, perform one extra conservative pass.

## Overclaim control and arbitration rules

### Mandatory downgrade triggers

Automatically downgrade claims when any of these are detected:
- observational evidence expressed as direct causality
- surrogate biomarker changes presented as proven clinical benefit
- single-center/small-sample findings generalized without boundaries
- cross-population extrapolation (adult→pediatric, CRSwNP→all CRS, etc.) without explicit limits
- “absence of evidence” rewritten as “evidence of absence”

### Arbitration rule for ambiguous sentences

When uncertain between two wording strengths:
1. choose the more conservative option by default
2. prefer latest high-quality guideline wording over narrative preference
3. add explicit scope limits (population, setting, endpoint, time horizon)

## Discussion-derived anti-AI checklist (applied per paragraph)

- Is the opening sentence data- or mechanism-anchored (not generic filler)?
- Does wording strength match the cited evidence level?
- Are mechanism and clinical implication linked explicitly and plausibly?
- Are extrapolation boundaries stated?
- Are banned hype words removed or replaced by measured alternatives?
- Is sentence rhythm varied naturally (no repetitive “This study...”, “Moreover...”, etc.)?

## Minimal publication-safe language bank

Prefer:
- "is associated with"
- "appears to contribute to"
- "is consistent with"
- "current evidence suggests"
- "may indicate"
- "in this population"

Avoid unless evidence is very strong and explicitly cited:
- "proves"
- "definitively demonstrates"
- "gold standard" (without guideline basis)
- "groundbreaking" / "revolutionary"
